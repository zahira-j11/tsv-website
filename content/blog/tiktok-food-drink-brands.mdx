Food and drink has a problem that most other categories don't have.

You can taste a skincare brand's product through a before-and-after. You can understand a fitness brand's results through transformation content. But you can't eat through a screen. You can't smell a sourdough loaf or feel the cold of a drink through a video. The sensory experience that drives purchase in food and drink is completely inaccessible through TikTok.

And yet some food and drink brands are doing extraordinarily well on the platform. Not just getting views but actually converting. Here is what they have figured out.

## The answer is not food porn

The first thing most food and drink brands try is aesthetic product content. Close-up shots of food that looks good. Steam rising from a coffee. Sauce being poured in slow motion. It performs terribly.

The problem is that beautiful food content is everywhere. Every restaurant, every consumer brand, every food blogger has been producing it for a decade. The audience is completely desensitised. A perfectly shot croissant does not stop the scroll in 2026 because it looks exactly like the last four hundred perfectly shot croissants.

The brands that are winning are doing something different. They are not trying to show you how good the product looks. They are trying to make you feel something about the product.

## Appetite is emotional, not visual

Rory Sutherland makes the point in [Alchemy](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/26210508-alchemy) that most of what we think we want is a rationalisation of what we actually feel. We don't eat certain things because they look good. We eat them because of associations, memories, identity signals, social meaning. The restaurant that's always booked out isn't better than its competitors on any measurable food quality metric. But it means something to be seen there, and that meaning is what people are actually buying.

The food and drink brands crushing it on TikTok have understood this intuitively. They are not selling the product. They are selling the feeling of being the kind of person who has this product in their life.

Fever-Tree doesn't post videos about how their tonic water is made. They post content about the ritual of making a good drink. The experience of a perfectly made G&T at the end of a long week. The social scene that surrounds it. The product is present, but it's the feeling that does the work.

## What actually converts: specificity over aesthetic

The content format that consistently converts for food and drink brands right now is the specific, opinionated recommendation.

Not "our coffee is great." Not a slow-motion pour. A video that says "this is the best coffee for cold brew and here is exactly why, which bean, which roast level, which grind size." Content that gives the viewer something specific to hold onto.

This works for the same reason it works in health and wellness: specificity is a [costly signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signalling_theory). You have to actually know what you're talking about to be that specific. Brands that can speak with genuine expertise about their category, not just their product, build a kind of trust that generic brand content never reaches.

The viewer who learns something useful from your content has received a gift. When they see your product in their search results later, or your creator recommending it, or your brand coming up in someone else's video, they already have a positive relationship with you. That relationship exists before any conscious decision to buy.

## Founders and provenance outperform everything else

The single best-performing content type we have seen for food and drink brands, consistently across categories, is provenance content: where the product comes from, how it is made, and specifically the person behind it.

Not in a corporate "brand story" sense. In a genuinely personal sense. The cheesemaker walking you through why they source milk from exactly those farms. The distillery founder explaining the decision to use a particular still. The baker showing you the starter culture that has been running since 2008.

This works because it answers the question that sits underneath every food and drink purchase: can I trust that this is what they say it is? The lab coat in a health brand is fake provenance. A founder walking through their actual production process is real provenance. The audience can tell the difference.

It also creates the parasocial bond that drives repeat purchase. People don't just buy the product. They buy it because they feel like they know the person who makes it, and they want that person to succeed.

## The format breakdown

From our campaigns in this category, these are the formats that consistently earn conversion, not just views:

**Recipe integration** that is genuinely useful, where the product is essential rather than decorative. Not "add our sauce to anything." A specific dish, a specific reason why it works there, a result the viewer actually wants to make.

**Taste reaction content** with real, specific descriptions. Not "mmm this is amazing." The exact flavour notes, the texture, the comparison to something the viewer knows. Give people the sensory language to want it, since they can't have the sensory experience yet.

**Category education** that positions your brand as the authority. If you make a premium product in a category most people don't understand, teach them what to look for. The viewer who now knows how to evaluate olive oil will buy yours because you're the brand that taught them.

**Behind the scenes with stakes**. Not a clean factory tour. A moment where something could go wrong, a harvest that depends on weather, a batch that didn't work and why. Stakes create investment, and investment creates memory.

---

The brands that treat TikTok as a place to put their TV ads in portrait mode will keep getting TV ad performance, which is to say: very little. The brands that figure out they're not selling a product, they're selling a feeling and a relationship, are the ones building something that compounds.

In food and drink, the question is never "how do we make this look good." It's "how do we make someone feel something about this that they didn't feel before they watched."
