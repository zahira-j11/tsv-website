A typical street interview shoot for us is one day in London. There is a presenter, a videographer and someone from our team on site. We speak to around 20 to 25 people over about five hours, and that gives us enough material for a batch of compilation videos. The day itself is the easy part to see. Most of the work happens before and after it.

This is how it actually runs.

## Before the shoot: the questions

Every month starts with a strategy call with the client. We pitch the concepts for the month, including the street interview questions, and the client approves them. We have written before about [which question formats work best](/blog/street-interview-marketing-uk). The short version is that the question is the video. A weak question cannot be saved in the edit.

The questions then go onto cue cards. We format them to fit on a phone rather than printing them, because the presenter is holding a microphone in one hand and needs to glance at the next question without breaking the conversation.

A shoot might have around 19 questions across a few cards. The presenter does not ask everyone all of them. Each card has a small number in the corner: the quota. That is how many people we need to answer that question for the video to work.

The quotas matter because a lot of our street interview videos are compilations. One video might use seven to ten different people's answers, depending on how good they are and how long each person talks. So we plan backwards from the number of videos we need.

## Casting the presenter

The presenter makes or breaks the day. We cast from our own creator network, and clients can review and approve the presenter before the shoot.

What we look for is simple to describe and hard to find: someone with no social anxiety. They are going to walk up to strangers for five hours and get turned down most of the time. They need to be warm, quick and completely unbothered by a "no".

We also think about how the presenter looks next to the audience. For a client talking to people about pensions, whose audience is mostly 30 to 40, we asked the presenter to dress business casual. Someone who looks like they belong in that crowd gets more people to stop.

## Picking where and when

Location decides who you meet, so we choose it based on who the client wants to hear from.

Some places have huge footfall but the wrong people. Covent Garden is busy all day, but a lot of the people walking through are tourists, and a tourist cannot tell you much about UK mortgages. Streets nearby with more residents and workers usually give us better answers for UK-specific questions. For a corporate audience we head towards the City. King's Cross is a good mix of commuters, office workers and people out for food.

Timing matters too. Weekday lunchtimes are good for working people. And we do not film in bad weather if we can avoid it. When it is raining, nobody wants to stop.

## On the day

We ask the presenter to arrive ten minutes early so we can get them mic'd up and walk through the cards.

The presenter and the videographer work on a signal. One of them gives a thumbs up when they are ready, and only then does the interview start. It sounds basic, but the most common problem on a shoot is the camera rolling too late, or not being in the right position when the conversation begins. You cannot ask a stranger to repeat a brilliant answer.

Someone from our team is always there. Partly to handle anything that comes up, and partly to keep an ear on the answers, so that by the end of the day we know every quota has been met.

## Consent comes first

Every time we stop someone, the presenter reads out a set line asking for their consent to be filmed and posted on social media, and we record their answer on camera. Anyone who says no does not appear in anything.

We do this at the start, not the end, for a practical reason. People occasionally agree to chat, hear the consent line, and walk away. That is their right, and it is much better to find out in the first ten seconds than after ten minutes of interview we cannot use.

## Getting answers you can actually use

Londoners are brilliant at giving the shortest answer possible. "Yeah." "Probably." "Dunno." A one-word answer is useless in a compilation, so the presenter's main job is to ask "why?" and get people to expand. The best videos come from the second sentence, not the first.

Questions with a number in them need a different push. If we ask "how much do you think you need to retire in the UK?" and someone says "loads", the presenter asks for a ballpark. Even a range is enough, because in the edit we can show how different people's answers compare.

Personal money questions are the hardest. Roughly half the people we ask are comfortable answering them on camera. In our experience, students and people near retirement are the most open. Students all have fairly chaotic finances and find it funny. Older people are looking back and happy to share what they wish they had done. People in the middle are the most guarded.

A few other things we have learned:

- Fun, trivia-style questions work because people walk away smiling, even if they were sceptical when they stopped.
- Small thank-yous help on harder briefs. We have handed out chocolates, and for student shoots, food vouchers.
- Do not over-brand the day. A big logo on the microphone makes people think they are being sold to, and they are more likely to say no.

## After the shoot

The footage goes to our editors, who cut the compilations and add captions. We deliver videos on a rolling basis rather than all at the end of the month. The client reviews each one on our approval platform and can approve it or leave notes. Then we schedule and post.

For a new client, the first content is usually live within 14 to 21 days of signing. Week one is strategy and casting, week two is production, and by week three the first batch is going out.

## Quick answers

**How long does a street interview shoot take?**
Usually around five hours for one day, with the presenter, a videographer and someone from our team.

**How many people do you need to interview?**
We aim for 20 to 25 people on a typical shoot. A single compilation video uses seven to ten answers.

**Do people need to give consent to be in a street interview video?**
Yes. We read a consent line to every person at the start and record their answer on camera. Anyone who declines is not used.

**Where are the best places in London to film street interviews?**
It depends on who you want to hear from. Tourist-heavy areas are busy but give fewer UK-relevant answers. Commuter and office areas like the City or King's Cross are better for most brand briefs.
