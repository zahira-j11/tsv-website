You know the type of health content that's everywhere on TikTok right now. Founder in a lab coat. Dramatic before-and-after. A claim that sounds just believable enough. Link in bio.

Most of it tanks. Some of it pops for about a week before the returns dry up and the chargebacks start coming in.

And yet some wellness brands are genuinely winning on TikTok. Not going viral once and disappearing. Actually winning. Steady views, audiences that come back, customers who buy a second time. I manage a handful of these accounts and the thing they share is not a big budget or a clever hook formula. It's that they understand something most brands don't about how people actually decide to trust something.

## The lab coat doesn't work anymore (and maybe never did)

Rory Sutherland has this idea in [Alchemy](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/26210508-alchemy) that most marketing fails because it tries to convince people rationally. The problem is that humans don't really work that way. Before we had contracts or credentials or consumer protection law, we had to figure out who to trust from behavioural cues: things that were hard to fake because they required genuine investment. He calls these [costly signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signalling_theory).

A lab coat is not a costly signal. It costs £40 and takes about thirty seconds to put on. The audience knows this. They've seen too many founders in lab coats making claims that turned out to be nothing. The signal has been devalued.

What's hard to fake is actual knowledge. Consistency over time. Giving people useful information without asking for anything in return. Those things require real effort, and that effort is exactly what makes them credible.

TikTok, the way it's structured, rewards this kind of thing and punishes the cheap version. That's not a coincidence. It's why it's become the most effective platform for the wellness brands that have figured this out.

## Opening on the product is the single fastest way to lose someone

Every health brand I've seen come to us with a TikTok problem has the same issue: they're leading with the product.

A bottle. A supplement sachet. "Introducing our new collagen formula." The viewer clocks it as an ad in the first half second and it's over.

The brands that are actually getting cut-through are opening on a problem or a question, something the viewer recognises or didn't know they needed to know. The magnesium brand that's killing it right now doesn't open with "our magnesium is different." They open with "most people in the UK are low in magnesium and have no idea. Here's how to check." The product shows up at the end almost like an aside.

It works because teaching someone something real is a costly signal. You have to actually know the thing to teach it. A brand that gives away useful information without immediately demanding a sale is doing something that only makes sense if they're confident the product is good. That confidence reads before anyone consciously registers the brand name.

Sutherland's framing for this is the difference between persuading someone and giving them a reason to persuade themselves. The second one sticks.

## People don't trust brands. They trust people.

A slick brand video with a logo intro almost always underperforms the same information delivered by a real person on their phone. Some of this is algorithmic. A lot of it is just how humans are wired.

There's a concept in media psychology called [parasocial relationships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasocial_interaction), the one-sided emotional bonds people form with people they watch regularly. Horton and Wohl first described it in 1956, and the social media research since then has been pretty consistent: these bonds trigger the same trust responses as real relationships. The brand logo triggers none of that.

A creator with 40k followers who actually uses the product and talks about it like a normal person will outperform a creator with 400k followers who's clearly reading from a brief. Every time. The size of the audience matters less than how much that audience genuinely trusts the person in front of them.

Authenticity isn't a vibe or a shooting style. It's whether they actually use the thing. Audiences are very good at knowing the difference even when they can't say why.

## The boring video is your best friend

This one takes people by surprise. The content that consistently converts in the wellness space is not the high-concept stuff. It's the plain, useful, slightly boring answer to a specific question.

"I took magnesium glycinate every night for 60 days. Here's what actually changed." That's not exciting. It's not trying to shock anyone. But it answers a question people are genuinely searching for, and it keeps showing up in search results for months.

This connects to something Robert Zajonc showed pretty conclusively in the 1960s: the [mere exposure effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mere-exposure_effect). We develop more positive feelings toward things we encounter repeatedly, even when we don't consciously remember the previous encounters. A brand that keeps appearing in the search results you're already using builds familiarity before you even consider buying.

One video with 3 million views and no repeat exposure is worth less, commercially, than 50 videos with 60k views each that keep showing up every time someone searches your category over the next year. The math on that is not close.

## Say the uncomfortable thing about your category

Wellness is full of noise. Every brand claiming to be the best, the most bioavailable, the most ethically sourced. After a while it all sounds the same.

The brands that actually cut through are the ones willing to say something most brands won't. The protein brand that posts "most protein powders are underdosing their key ingredients and it won't show results. Here's what to look for on the label" is doing something risky. They're criticising the category they're part of.

It's uncomfortable. It's also immediately more credible than anything a lifestyle photoshoot could do.

Sutherland's point on this in Alchemy is that the most persuasive thing you can do is behave in a way that would be irrational if you were bad at what you do. Pointing out flaws in your category, sharing information that costs you in the short term, being transparent when you don't have to be. These are all behaviours that only make sense if you're genuinely confident. The brain reads that at a level below conscious reasoning.

Having a real point of view isn't the same as being controversial. It's just being honest about what you actually believe. That's what earns the kind of audience that trusts you when it counts.

## TikTok search is where purchase decisions start

More people are using TikTok to research health questions than most brands realise. "Best supplements for sleep." "Does collagen actually do anything." "What's actually causing my bloating." These aren't small searches.

The brands showing up consistently in those results, with genuinely useful answers, not just product pitches, are accumulating trust in the exact context where buying decisions happen. Someone who finds you three times while searching three different things isn't a cold lead by the time they hit your profile. They've already formed a view about whether you know what you're talking about.

The brands building that presence right now are going to be hard to displace. Not because they have more followers. Because they've got more of the right trust, in the right places, built up over time.

---

If you're running a wellness brand and TikTok feels like it's not working, it's usually one of these things. You're opening on the product. You're using branded content instead of real people. Or you're optimising for the viral moment instead of the consistent presence.

The fix isn't a better hook formula. It's thinking about trust the way your audience does: not something you declare, but something they decide for themselves.
