If you have spent any time watching short-form content in the last two years, you have seen the format. Someone stops a stranger on a busy London street, asks them something about money, careers, or their life, and the stranger's answer gets three million views.

That is [street interview marketing](/street-interviews). And for UK brands, it is currently one of the most efficient ways to generate awareness at scale without paying for ads.

We know this because we have run it for clients across finance, health, and lifestyle. Here is everything we have learned.

## Why street interviews work

Most branded content fails because it feels like branded content. The viewer's brain recognises the format and skips before the first second is up.

Street interviews break that pattern. The format signals authenticity. A real person, on a real street, giving an unscripted answer to a question that genuinely interests them. The brand is the context, not the content.

The psychological mechanism is social proof at scale. When someone watches a stranger answer "How much do you spend in a month?" honestly and without embarrassment, they feel permission to wonder the same thing. The brand that asked the question gets associated with the insight, without ever having to claim authority.

There is something else happening too, which is less discussed. Branded content tries to persuade. Street interviews do not try to persuade anyone. They just let people talk. And because there is no persuasion attempt, the viewer's defences never go up. You cannot skip something that is not selling you anything.

### The share is the real metric

Paid content works by interrupting someone and hoping your message lands before they skip. Street interview organic content works by becoming something people choose to watch, and share.

The share is the key multiplier. A polished brand ad gets zero shares. A short interview where someone says something genuinely surprising about their savings habits gets shared in group chats and reposted. That secondary distribution is free, and it tends to be worth more than the original view.

### The algorithm loves it

Short-form algorithms are optimised for watch time and completion rate. Street interviews routinely outperform [polished brand content](/blog/lo-fi-vs-high-production-video) on both metrics.

The hook is inherent: the question is the hook. Answers vary from person to person, so your audience does not tune out. And the conversation format triggers completion, because humans are wired to hear the end of a conversation. We feel genuinely uncomfortable stopping halfway through someone's sentence.

It is the format behind most of our biggest results. Street interviews about buying a home took [Habito](/case-studies/habito) to 25 million organic views in 8 months with zero ad spend, and made up about 95% of what we posted for them. [Uni Compare](/case-studies/uni-compare) passed 1 million views in its first 8 weeks, and [TALAB](/case-studies/talab) grew from launch to 6,000 users.

## The formats that work best

Not all street interview formats perform equally. Based on our campaigns, these are the ones that consistently generate strong reach.

### Compilation or single answer?

Every street interview video is one of two shapes. A compilation puts lots of answers to one question together, with the best ones up front to build tension. A single-person video follows one answer from start to finish, which works when the story is in how one person thinks it through.

The questions fall into two types too. Data-driven questions take a statistic and turn it into a guessing game: ask people which degrees produce the most millionaires, then reveal the answer. Opinion-driven questions, like a "this or that" or a hot take, get people arguing in the comments. The three formats below use both.

### The knowledge gap

Ask a question that exposes a gap between what people think they know and what they actually know.

*"Do you know what APR means?"*
*"What do you think the average UK mortgage rate is right now?"*
*"What percentage of your income do you save each month?"*

The answer does not have to be embarrassing. It has to be honest. Honesty is what drives shares.

### The opinion divide

Ask something where people genuinely disagree, and let the camera capture that disagreement.

*"Is renting a waste of money?"*
*"Should couples have separate bank accounts?"*
*"Is a university degree worth it in 2026?"*

These generate comments, which algorithms treat as engagement signals. More comments means more distribution.

### The reveal

Ask a question, get an answer, then reveal something surprising.

*"What would you do with an extra 500 pounds a month?"*
[Person answers]
*"What if I told you that switching your mortgage provider could save you exactly that?"*

This is the format that ties most directly to what you sell, because it draws a line from the content to the value proposition. It is also harder to execute. The reveal has to feel earned, not scripted.

## How to do it in the UK

Street interviewing in the UK is different from the US, where the format originated. British people are more reserved. They are less likely to stop, less likely to overshare, and more likely to give one-word answers. These are all solvable problems.

### Location matters more than you think

**Canary Wharf** works for finance and career content. The crowd skews professional, opinionated, and once they stop, willing to talk. Avoid Mondays before 9am and Fridays after 4pm.

**Shoreditch and Brick Lane** work for lifestyle, fashion, and food brands. Creative industries, younger demographic, higher stop rate.

**Waterloo and London Bridge** give you mixed demographics, commuters of all ages. Good for broadly applicable questions about money or lifestyle.

**Covent Garden** has the highest stop rate but the lowest conversion to interesting answers. Too many tourists, less stake in UK-specific questions.

### The interviewer is everything

This is not a job for your marketing assistant. The person holding the microphone needs to be warm, quick, and comfortable with silence. They need to know when to ask a follow-up and when to let the answer breathe.

Former journalists, improv comedians, and experienced podcasters make the best street interviewers. The skill is interviewing, not presenting.

### Consent and legal

In the UK, filming people in public is generally legal, but using their likeness in commercial content requires consent. We use recorded consent at the start of each interview, captured on camera before filming begins. Anyone who declines does not appear in the final content.

## Connecting it to your brand

The most common mistake brands make with street interviews is over-branding. They put their logo on every frame, reference their product in every question, and wonder why the content performs like an ad. Because it is one.

The right approach is ambient branding. Your brand is present but not intrusive.

- A branded microphone flag or windshield, subtle but consistent
- A brand-relevant question, you are the mortgage company asking about mortgages
- A caption or overlay that links the insight back to your product

The viewer should not feel sold to. They should feel like they have learned something, and then discover that you are the brand that knows this stuff.

Street interviews are not a hack. They work because they are genuinely useful. They give viewers something real: honest answers to questions they are slightly afraid to ask. The brand that facilitates that honesty earns the association.

## Quick answers

**What is street interview marketing?**
Short-form videos where a brand stops people on the street and films their answers to a question about its category. The brand is the context, not the content, so people watch it as entertainment, not an ad.

**Why do street interviews work on TikTok, Reels and Shorts?**
The question is the hook, and answers vary from person to person. People also want to hear the end of a conversation. That drives watch time, completion and shares, which short-form algorithms reward.

**Where should you film street interviews in London?**
Canary Wharf for finance and careers. Shoreditch and Brick Lane for lifestyle, fashion and food. Waterloo and London Bridge for a mixed crowd. Covent Garden stops the most people but gives the fewest useful answers.

**Is it legal to film street interviews in the UK?**
Filming people in public is generally legal, but using someone's likeness in commercial content needs their consent. We record consent on camera before each interview, and anyone who declines doesn't appear.

**How much branding should a street interview have?**
Very little: a branded mic flag, a question that fits your category, and a caption that links the insight to your product. Logos on every frame make it perform like an ad.

**What types of street interview videos are there?**
Compilations, which cut lots of answers to one question together, and single-person videos, which follow one answer from start to finish. Questions are either data-driven guessing games or opinion-driven debates.
